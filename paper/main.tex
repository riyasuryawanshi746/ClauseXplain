\begin{abstract}
Public contract-understanding resources are almost entirely based on US agreements, yet practitioners in India work with contracts governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and related statutes. We study how well a Longformer model trained only on CUAD transfers to Indian contracts. We map CUAD's 41 clause types to an 18-category Indian-context taxonomy (12 categories have training data), train a multi-task Longformer-base with a clause classifier and a question-conditioned evidence-span head, and evaluate it on a new hand-labelled set of 334 clauses from 13 Indian documents. In 3-fold contract-grouped cross-validation on CUAD the classifier reaches 0.84 macro-F1, but on the 192 Indian clauses in categories it was trained on, accuracy falls to 0.35 and macro-F1 to 0.34, indistinguishable from a TF-IDF baseline (0.33 and 0.34). A LEGAL-BERT classifier, stronger on CUAD (0.87 macro-F1), drops to the same level (accuracy 0.36, macro-F1 0.34). On CUAD the span head is about 24$\times$ faster and 0.125 token-F1 better than scanning candidate spans with the classifier, but on Indian text it is only marginally better than a lexical baseline (token-F1 0.32 vs.\ 0.30; random 0.10). Training on Indian clauses with whole documents held out closes part of the gap: a LEGAL-BERT classifier first trained on CUAD and then on the Indian clauses reaches 0.57 accuracy and 0.52 macro-F1 on all 334 clauses (mean of three seeds; 0.59 macro-F1 on the 192 seen-category clauses) and learns the categories CUAD lacks. Adding LEDGAR provisions mapped to our categories raises accuracy to 0.60 and improves 13 of 17 categories, and InLegalBERT, pretrained on Indian judgments, does not beat LEGAL-BERT. Adding 454 double-annotated clauses from 15 private-sector Indian agreements to the training data, with the test clauses unchanged, raises macro-F1 to 0.61 (0.64 with LEDGAR; one seed), mostly in the categories CUAD lacks. We also report where the model fails and describe a rule layer that attaches candidate Indian-statute flags for human review. The Indian set is small, mostly drawn from government documents, and its labels only moderately reliable ($\kappa=0.65$ on a 100-unit sample), so we present the results as a domain-shift study and benchmark seed, not as a deployable system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Automated contract review is a well-studied task in legal NLP, driven by datasets such as CUAD \cite{hendrycks2021cuad}. These datasets are drawn from US filings, whereas Indian contracts follow different drafting conventions and statutes (for example the Indian Contract Act, 1872; the MSMED Act, 2006; and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023). It is unclear how much a model trained on US contracts transfers.

This paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A mapping from CUAD's 41 clause types to an 18-category Indian-context taxonomy, with an explicit account of which categories have training data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item A multi-task Longformer with a clause classifier and an evidence-span head (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}), with a controlled comparison against single-task models, a LEGAL-BERT classifier and lexical baselines on CUAD (Section~\ref{sec:cuad}).
\item A measurement of domain shift on a new set of 334 hand-labelled Indian clauses (plus an 11-clause slice quoted in court judgments), including what the model predicts for categories it never saw (Section~\ref{sec:indian}).
\item Document-held-out training on the Indian set, showing that CUAD pretraining followed by Indian training is needed for the gain, and testing LEDGAR and private-sector Indian clauses as extra data and InLegalBERT as the encoder (Section~\ref{sec:adapt}).
\item A comparison of the span head with scanning candidate spans using the classifier, showing a large accuracy and speed advantage (Section~\ref{sec:scan}).
\end{enumerate}
We deliberately report negative and uncertain results (for example, no reliable gain over TF-IDF out of domain) and list limitations in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Contract datasets.} CUAD \cite{hendrycks2021cuad} provides 510 commercial contracts from SEC filings with over 13,000 expert annotations in 41 clause types and is the source of our training data. LEDGAR \cite{tuggener2020ledgar} labels about 100,000 provisions from more than 60,000 SEC-filed contracts with provision types, and ContractNLI \cite{koreeda2021contractnli} frames review of 607 non-disclosure agreements as document-level inference with evidence spans. LexGLUE \cite{chalkidis2022lexglue} groups LEDGAR with other English legal tasks into one benchmark. All of these are drawn from US or other Western filings, so they say little about contracts drafted under Indian law.

\textbf{Legal language models and domain shift.} LEGAL-BERT \cite{chalkidis2020legalbert} pretrains BERT on legal text; we use it as a classification baseline. Longformer \cite{beltagy2020longformer} handles long inputs, which our span head needs (windows of up to 1,024 tokens). Continued pretraining on in-domain text is a standard remedy for domain shift \cite{gururangan2020dontstop}, and InLegalBERT \cite{paul2023inlegalbert} applies it to Indian court judgments. We test InLegalBERT as an encoder but do no further pretraining ourselves (Section~\ref{sec:adapt}).

\textbf{Indian legal NLP.} Indian legal NLP has mostly studied court judgments: judgment prediction and explanation (ILDC) \cite{malik2021ildc}, rhetorical-role segmentation \cite{kalamkar2022rhetorical}, and the IL-TUR benchmark of judgment-centred tasks \cite{joshi2024iltur}. Work on Indian contracts is scarce. ICAT \cite{icat} collects contract clauses quoted in about 60 Indian judgments; like our judgment slice, it sees contracts only through litigation. LAUKIN \cite{singh2026laukin} pairs clauses from Australian, UK and Indian contracts for legal-equivalence classification and reports that drafting conventions differ across these jurisdictions. We found no public dataset that labels clause categories with evidence spans in Indian contract documents, or that measures how CUAD-trained models transfer to them; ours is a small first step.

\section{Data and Taxonomy}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{CUAD mapping}
CUAD v1 has 510 contracts. We map its clause types to 18 categories (Assignment, Commercial Terms, Confidentiality, Dispute Resolution, Force Majeure, Governing Law/Jurisdiction, Indemnification, Intellectual Property, Liability, Modification, Non-Compete, Non-Solicitation, Other, Payment, Penalties, Privacy/Data Protection, Termination, Warranty). Of CUAD's 13,823 annotated spans, 9,786 from 481 contracts map to one of these categories and form our training data; 3,998 metadata spans (titles, parties, dates) and 39 excluded spans are dropped. Only 12 categories receive any CUAD data; Confidentiality, Force Majeure, Indemnification, Modification, Payment and Privacy/Data Protection have none, so the model cannot output them. Dispute Resolution is represented only by CUAD's ``Covenant Not To Sue'' type (173 spans), which is a narrow slice of what Indian arbitration and dispute clauses contain.

\subsection{Indian evaluation set}
The set was built in two pools from 13 publicly available Indian documents; every clause has exact character offsets in its source document.

\textbf{Original pool (228 clauses, 11 documents).} Government RFP/consultancy agreements, a unified telecom licence, concession agreements, a non-disclosure agreement, and general terms and conditions. Each clause was assigned one of the 18 categories by one annotator using written definitions and tie-break rules. A second annotator independently labelled a random sample of 100 units (18 of which the first annotator marked as not a clause) without seeing the first labels; raw agreement was 0.68 and Cohen's $\kappa=0.65$ (agreement 0.73, $\kappa=0.71$ on the 79 units both treated as clauses). The 32 disagreements are listed in the repository; most involve the catch-all category Other, Commercial Terms, and whether a unit is a clause at all. The disagreements were adjudicated blind (the two labels shown in random order without annotator identity): the final label matched the primary annotator in 21 cases and the second annotator in 11, changing 11 primary labels. All Indian results below use the adjudicated labels. Neither annotator has legal training: the primary annotator is the first author, and the second annotator is a computer-engineering undergraduate who worked only from the written guidelines. Agreement therefore measures how consistently the guidelines can be applied by non-experts, not expert legal judgement. Clause selection was partly keyword-assisted and is therefore biased toward typical wording.

\textbf{Batch 2 (106 clauses, 2 documents).} The CPWD General Conditions of Contract 2023 (text extracted from PDF, with some garbled lines) and a draft waste-to-compost concession agreement containing bracketed placeholders. Candidate units were selected by category keywords and labelled by the primary annotator only; no unit was marked as not a clause, so some definitions and noisy units remain. On 53\% of units the label equals the keyword category that selected the unit. This pool has no second-annotator check.

In total, 192 of the 334 clauses fall in categories with CUAD training data (including Other) and 142 in the six unseen categories. Documents are unevenly sized (the largest contributes 67 clauses), and nearly all are government documents.

\textbf{Judgment slice (11 clauses).} Contract clauses quoted verbatim in five Indian court judgments (Indian Kanoon), mostly non-compete, non-solicitation, arbitration and termination clauses. They were hand-picked, are kept separate from the main set, and are scored for classification only.

\textbf{Batch 3 (454 clauses, 15 documents; extra training data).} Private-sector agreements governed by Indian law: employment and master services agreements filed as SEC exhibits, share purchase, shareholders' and business-transfer agreements and a trust-deed amendment from Indian stock-exchange filings, a university MoU, and two Maharashtra leave-and-license deeds. Units were sampled at random (at most 45 per document group), with a keyword-selected top-up for weak categories (69 of the 454 clauses); near-duplicates across documents sharing a template were skipped. Both annotators labelled all 523 units independently (raw agreement 0.62, $\kappa=0.57$). We then revised the guideline (mainly Other versus Commercial Terms, and party and signature blocks as non-clauses), and each annotator re-labelled the 200 disagreements seeing only their own earlier label ($\kappa=0.73$ over all 523 units; the 323 agreed units were not re-checked, so this is optimistic). The 127 remaining disagreements were adjudicated blind; the final label matched the primary annotator in 111, the second annotator in 7 and neither in 9, so despite blinding these labels lean towards the primary annotator. 69 units were judged not to be clauses. Batch 3 is used only as additional training data, so test results stay comparable on the 334 clauses.

\subsection{Model}
The encoder is Longformer-base-4096 \cite{beltagy2020longformer}. The classifier head predicts a category from a clause (maximum 512 tokens). The span head takes a category-specific question and a 3,000-character window of the contract (maximum 1,024 tokens) and predicts start/end logits over the window. An ordinal risk head exists in the code but is not used in any reported result. We compare three variants: classifier only, span only, and joint training. As a classification baseline we fine-tune LEGAL-BERT-base \cite{chalkidis2020legalbert} (\texttt{nlpaueb/legal-bert-base-uncased}, 110M parameters, 512 tokens) as a classifier only, with the same splits and settings; it has no span head.

\subsection{Training and protocol}
All CUAD experiments use contract-grouped 5-fold splits so no contract appears in both training and test data; we ran folds 0--2. Settings: 2 epochs, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ (heads $10^{-4}$), seed 13, mixed precision on a T4 GPU, span training on 50\% of eligible items. Baselines: majority class, TF-IDF with logistic regression (trained on the same split), a whole-window span, a lexical-centroid span, and a random span.

\subsection{Rule layer}
A rule engine applies hand-written features (e.g., whether a duration or cap is stated) to confidently classified clauses and raises candidate flags tied to the Indian Contract Act (ss.\,23, 27, 28, 39, 56, 73--75, 124--125), the MSMED Act (ss.\,15--16) and the DPDPA (ss.\,6, 8). Flags are prompts for human review, not legal findings. The 15 rules have not been reviewed by a lawyer, so we make no claim about their legal correctness and report no accuracy for them; we describe the layer only as a review aid.

\section{Results on CUAD}\label{sec:cuad}
Table~\ref{tab:cuad} gives means over three folds (about 2,030 test clauses per fold). The Longformer classifiers beat TF-IDF by 0.036 to 0.037 macro-F1 with fold-level intervals above zero, but the folds are few. Joint training did not change classification (difference $-0.001$, interval includes zero). LEGAL-BERT is the strongest classifier (0.866 macro-F1, +0.027 over the Longformer classifier; its lowest fold, 0.854, is above the Longformer mean), and trains in about 3.3 minutes per fold. A second run with the same seed and splits gave 0.870, so GPU non-determinism alone moves this number by about 0.005. Clause classification therefore does not need Longformer's long context; we keep Longformer for the span head. For span localisation, the joint model reaches 0.760 token-F1 against 0.604 for the lexical-centroid baseline.

\section{Domain Shift to Indian Contracts}\label{sec:indian}

\textbf{Findings.} (i) Macro-F1 falls from 0.84 on CUAD to about 0.34, consistently across checkpoints. (ii) Out of domain, the classifier is no better than TF-IDF (accuracy 0.347 vs.\ 0.333, macro-F1 0.338 vs.\ 0.344), and neither is LEGAL-BERT (0.356, 0.342) despite its higher CUAD score, so the drop is not specific to the Longformer encoder; LEGAL-BERT sends unseen categories to the same neighbours. (iii) Batch 2 is harder than the original pool (0.275 vs.\ 0.382), but with 63 clauses the intervals overlap (fold 0: 0.16--0.37 vs.\ 0.28--0.44); keyword selection, retained noisy units and PDF noise are possible causes we have not separated. (iv) On Indian text the span head's advantage over the lexical baseline nearly disappears (0.020 token-F1, against 0.156 on CUAD), although its exact match stays well above the baselines. (v) Per category (fold 0), Penalties (F1 0.56) and Termination (0.53) transfer best; Dispute Resolution does not (F1 0.00 on 18 clauses), consistent with its narrow CUAD coverage, and the catch-all Other is mostly misclassified (F1 0.22 on 45). (vi) For the 142 clauses in unseen categories, predictions go to the nearest trained category (fold 0): 20 of 43 Payment clauses become Commercial Terms, 15 of 19 Indemnification clauses become Liability, and 33 of 46 Confidentiality clauses become Other. Per-document accuracy varies (0.14--0.78 for documents with at least five clauses in trained categories), but most documents have fewer than 15 clauses in trained categories, so we do not draw conclusions per document.

\textbf{Judgment slice.} On the 11 judgment-quoted clauses, Longformer accuracy is 0.606 (folds: 0.455, 0.727, 0.636), LEGAL-BERT 0.545 and TF-IDF 0.667; with $n=11$ hand-picked clauses, mostly restrictive covenants, this is reported for completeness only.

\section{Training on Indian Clauses}\label{sec:adapt}
We next train on the Indian set itself, holding out whole documents: leave-one-group-out over 12 groups (the 13 documents, with the two MEA agreements, which share a template, held out together). Every one of the 334 clauses is predicted once by a model that never saw its document, and all 18 categories can be predicted (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}). Settings were fixed before the runs: 8 epochs, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ (head $10^{-4}$), batch 16, 512 tokens, last-epoch model, no selection on held-out data. We compare training on Indian clauses only with starting from a CUAD-trained checkpoint (fold 0) before training on Indian clauses; for TF-IDF the second setting adds all CUAD spans to the training data. We also test inverse-frequency class weights with label smoothing 0.1 and layer-wise learning-rate decay 0.9 (balanced class weights for TF-IDF).

Two further changes address the data and the encoder. \emph{Extra data:} LEDGAR \cite{tuggener2020ledgar} (LexGLUE training split, 60,000 SEC provisions) has labels for several categories CUAD lacks. We map its labels to our categories using only the definitions in our annotation guidelines (for example Amendments, Waivers and Entire Agreements to Modification; Payments, Fees and Taxes to Payment), fixed before any result was seen; 24,737 provisions map to 11 categories, and at most 100 per category are added to every training fold. LEDGAR has no Force Majeure, Privacy/Data Protection, Liability, Penalties, Non-Compete, Non-Solicitation or Commercial Terms label, and most of it comes from credit and employment agreements. \emph{Encoder:} InLegalBERT \cite{paul2023inlegalbert}, LEGAL-BERT further pretrained on Indian court judgments, in place of LEGAL-BERT with the same settings. \emph{More Indian data:} we add batch 3 to the training data, holding it out by document as well (23 groups; the three groups of documents that share a template or transaction are held out together). The 334 test clauses are predicted as before, but every fold's training data now also contains the 454 batch 3 clauses. The main neural configurations were run with three seeds (13--15); the batch 3 runs, at about 1.5 hours each, with seed 13 only. Table~\ref{tab:adapt} gives the results.

\textbf{Findings.} (i) CUAD pretraining followed by Indian training gives the best macro-F1: over three seeds LEGAL-BERT reaches $0.565\pm0.007$ accuracy and $0.524\pm0.006$ macro-F1 on all 334 clauses, and on the same 192 seen-category clauses its macro-F1 rises from 0.342 zero-shot to 0.593 (Fig.~\ref{fig:gain}). The categories absent from CUAD become learnable (Fig.~\ref{fig:percat}: Force Majeure F1 0.92, Confidentiality 0.75, Indemnification 0.70, Payment 0.51), except Modification and Privacy/Data Protection (0.00 on 7 and 8 clauses). (ii) Both data sources are needed: trained on Indian clauses alone, LEGAL-BERT is weak on the seen categories (macro-F1 0.231). (iii) LEDGAR raises LEGAL-BERT accuracy from 0.565 to 0.600 and improves F1 in 13 of 17 categories (Fig.~\ref{fig:percat}; e.g., Confidentiality 0.75$\rightarrow$0.82, Payment 0.51$\rightarrow$0.62, Modification 0.00$\rightarrow$0.23), but the paired bootstrap interval for the accuracy gain excludes zero for only one of the three seeds. Overall macro-F1 looks flat (0.512 vs.\ 0.524) mainly because Non-Solicitation has a single clause, whose F1 falls from 1.00 to 0.00; without it, macro-F1 rises from 0.494 to 0.544. Other and Force Majeure also lose F1. For TF-IDF it helps more clearly (macro-F1 0.351$\rightarrow$0.457 on Indian data, 0.427$\rightarrow$0.489 with CUAD). (iv) InLegalBERT is not better than LEGAL-BERT ($0.537\pm0.009$ accuracy, $0.450\pm0.007$ macro-F1; on CUAD fold 0, 0.843 vs.\ 0.864 macro-F1), so pretraining on Indian judgments does not carry over to contract clauses here. (v) Class weights, label smoothing and layer-wise decay did not help with these fixed settings, and LEGAL-BERT trained on Indian clauses alone with them failed to train (accuracy 0.249, most clauses predicted as Non-Solicitation), so we do not count that run as evidence about the techniques. (vi) Batch 2 remains harder (LEGAL-BERT accuracy 0.462 vs.\ 0.613 on the original pool), and the main confusions are Confidentiality$\rightarrow$Other, Payment$\rightarrow$Commercial Terms and Privacy$\rightarrow$Confidentiality; the first two involve Other and Commercial Terms, the categories most annotator disagreements involved. Seeds move accuracy by 0.01--0.02, groups are unequal (2 to 73 clauses), and many configurations are compared on the same clauses, so these numbers estimate what adaptation buys rather than a tuned result. (vii) More Indian training data gives the largest gain. With batch 3 added, LEGAL-BERT reaches 0.611 accuracy and 0.609 macro-F1 on the same 334 test clauses (seed 13; without batch 3, seed 13: 0.569 and 0.527). The paired bootstrap interval for the macro-F1 gain excludes zero against each of the three earlier seeds (+0.08 to +0.09), and for the accuracy gain (+0.04 to +0.05) against one. With LEDGAR as well it reaches 0.644 and 0.636 (macro-F1 +0.10 to +0.14 over the three LEDGAR seeds without batch 3, all intervals excluding zero; accuracy for two of three). The gain comes mostly from the categories CUAD lacks (unseen macro-F1 0.511$\rightarrow$0.732; seed 13: Modification 0.00$\rightarrow$0.33, Privacy/Data Protection 0.00$\rightarrow$0.50, Confidentiality 0.75$\rightarrow$0.87), while seen-category macro-F1 rises only from 0.593 to 0.614. TF-IDF moves the same way (0.482$\rightarrow$0.536 accuracy, 0.351$\rightarrow$0.469 macro-F1). These runs have one seed, and batch 3 was labelled under the revised guideline with labels that lean towards the primary annotator. The best accuracy, 0.64, is still below the 0.68 raw agreement between annotators. On the 11 judgment-quoted clauses the adapted models score 0.64--0.73.

\section{Span Head versus Classifier Scan}\label{sec:scan}
To locate a clause type in a 3,000-character window one can either run the span head once or split the window into about 50 sentence-aligned candidates and score each with the category classifier. On 500 test windows per fold (T4 GPU), the span head achieves a mean token-F1 of 0.756 against 0.630 for the scan (differences of +0.132, +0.142, +0.102, each with a paired-bootstrap 95\% interval excluding zero), at 54\,ms against about 1,314\,ms per window (about 24$\times$ faster). The two methods use different checkpoints by design, and three folds are too few for a cross-fold interval.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item The Indian set is small (334 clauses, 13 documents, almost all government documents), partly keyword-selected, labelled by one primary annotator without legal training (second-annotator $\kappa=0.65$ on 100 units of the original pool; none for batch 2), and partly OCR/PDF-noisy (the airport concession agreement and the CPWD GCC). Batch 3, used only for training, was labelled under a revised guideline, and its adjudicated labels lean towards the primary annotator; the batch 3 runs have a single seed.
\item Only 12 of 18 categories are learnable from CUAD; zero-shot, the system cannot recognise the others. Document-held-out training (Section~\ref{sec:adapt}) used settings fixed in advance, without a separate validation set; only the main configurations have three seeds.
\item Training was short (2 epochs, 50\% of span items); only 3 of 5 folds and a single seed were run.
\item Folds on the Indian set reuse the same clauses and are not independent.
\item The rule layer is unreviewed by a lawyer; outputs are not legal advice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Next Steps}\label{sec:next}
A CUAD-trained Longformer loses about 60\% of its macro-F1 on Indian contracts and does not beat TF-IDF there; a LEGAL-BERT classifier that is stronger on CUAD behaves the same. Its span head is fast and accurate on CUAD, but on Indian text it is only marginally better than a lexical baseline. Fine-tuning on Indian clauses with documents held out, starting from a CUAD-trained LEGAL-BERT, recovers much of the gap (0.52 macro-F1 over all 18 categories, 0.59 on the seen ones, over three seeds) and learns the categories CUAD lacks. Mapped LEDGAR provisions raise accuracy to 0.60 by helping those categories, and an encoder pretrained on Indian judgments does not help, which suggests that labelled Indian contract clauses matter more than Indian legal text in general. Adding 454 private-sector Indian clauses to training supports this: it gives the largest gain we observed (macro-F1 0.61, or 0.64 with LEDGAR, on the same test clauses; one seed). All results remain below the annotators' raw agreement. The Indian set is best read as a benchmark seed. Future work: grow the test set with private-sector contracts, which so far are used only for training, with at least 40 clauses per category; repeat the batch 3 runs with more seeds; double-annotate more units, including batch 2, and tighten the guideline where annotators disagree (Other, Commercial Terms, and whether a unit is a clause); clean the noisy units from the test set; continue pretraining on Indian contract text rather than judgments; have the rule layer reviewed by a lawyer; and complete all five CUAD folds with several seeds.

\section*{Availability}
Code, the CUAD category mapping, per-fold results and the annotation tooling are available at \url{https://github.com/riyasuryawanshi746/ClauseXplain}. The Indian documents are publicly available government publications; the repository includes their extracted text, the annotations (labels with character offsets into that text) and the adjudication records. For batch 3, the extracted text of 13 of the 15 documents is included with personal identifiers (PAN, Aadhaar, telephone, e-mail and account numbers) masked, together with the labels of their 435 batch 3 clauses; the two leave-and-license deeds, which are between private individuals, and their 19 clauses are not released, so the public training set has 769 of the 788 clauses used here. The judgment slice cites each judgment's public Indian Kanoon page and quotes only the contract clause.
