\begin{abstract}
Public contract-understanding resources are almost entirely based on US agreements, yet practitioners in India work with contracts governed by the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and related statutes. We study how well a Longformer model trained only on CUAD transfers to Indian contracts. We map CUAD's 41 clause types to an 18-category Indian-context taxonomy (12 categories have training data), train a multi-task Longformer-base with a clause classifier and a question-conditioned evidence-span head, and evaluate it on a new hand-labelled set of 334 clauses from 13 Indian documents. In 3-fold contract-grouped cross-validation on CUAD the classifier reaches 0.84 macro-F1, but on the 192 Indian clauses in categories it was trained on, accuracy falls to 0.34 and macro-F1 to 0.34, indistinguishable from a TF-IDF baseline (0.34 and 0.35). A LEGAL-BERT classifier, stronger on CUAD (0.87 macro-F1), drops to the same level (accuracy 0.35). On CUAD the span head is about 24$\times$ faster and 0.125 token-F1 better than scanning candidate spans with the classifier, but on Indian text it is only marginally better than a lexical baseline (token-F1 0.32 vs.\ 0.31; random 0.11). We also report where the model fails (e.g., Dispute Resolution, and six categories absent from CUAD) and describe a rule layer that attaches candidate Indian-statute flags for human review. The Indian set is small, mostly drawn from government documents, and its labels only moderately reliable ($\kappa=0.65$ on a 100-unit sample), so we present the results as a domain-shift study and benchmark seed, not as a deployable system.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Automated contract review is a well-studied task in legal NLP, driven by datasets such as CUAD \cite{hendrycks2021cuad}. These datasets are drawn from US filings, whereas Indian contracts follow different drafting conventions and statutes (for example the Indian Contract Act, 1872; the MSMED Act, 2006; and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023). It is unclear how much a model trained on US contracts transfers.

This paper makes four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A mapping from CUAD's 41 clause types to an 18-category Indian-context taxonomy, with an explicit account of which categories have training data (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
\item A multi-task Longformer with a clause classifier and an evidence-span head, with a controlled comparison against single-task models, a LEGAL-BERT classifier and lexical baselines on CUAD (Section~\ref{sec:cuad}).
\item A measurement of domain shift on a new set of 334 hand-labelled Indian clauses (plus an 11-clause slice quoted in court judgments), including what the model predicts for categories it never saw (Section~\ref{sec:indian}).
\item A comparison of the span head with scanning candidate spans using the classifier, showing a large accuracy and speed advantage (Section~\ref{sec:scan}).
\end{enumerate}
We deliberately report negative and uncertain results (for example, no reliable gain over TF-IDF out of domain) and list limitations in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Data and Taxonomy}\label{sec:data}
\subsection{CUAD mapping}
CUAD v1 has 510 contracts. We map its clause types to 18 categories (Assignment, Commercial Terms, Confidentiality, Dispute Resolution, Force Majeure, Governing Law/Jurisdiction, Indemnification, Intellectual Property, Liability, Modification, Non-Compete, Non-Solicitation, Other, Payment, Penalties, Privacy/Data Protection, Termination, Warranty). After mapping, 13,823 annotated spans from 510 contracts remain. Only 12 categories receive any CUAD data; Confidentiality, Force Majeure, Indemnification, Modification, Payment and Privacy/Data Protection have none, so the model cannot output them. Dispute Resolution is represented only by CUAD's ``Covenant Not To Sue'' type (173 spans), which is a narrow slice of what Indian arbitration and dispute clauses contain.

\subsection{Indian evaluation set}
The set was built in two pools from 13 publicly available Indian documents; every clause has exact character offsets in its source document.

\textbf{Original pool (228 clauses, 11 documents).} Government RFP/consultancy agreements, a unified telecom licence, concession agreements, a non-disclosure agreement, and general terms and conditions. Each clause was assigned one of the 18 categories by one annotator using written definitions and tie-break rules. A second annotator independently labelled a random sample of 100 units (18 of which the first annotator marked as not a clause) without seeing the first labels; raw agreement was 0.68 and Cohen's $\kappa=0.65$ (agreement 0.73, $\kappa=0.71$ on the 79 units both treated as clauses). The 32 disagreements are listed in the repository; most involve the catch-all category Other, Commercial Terms, and whether a unit is a clause at all. The disagreements were adjudicated blind (the two labels shown in random order without annotator identity): the final label matched the primary annotator in 21 cases and the second annotator in 11, changing 11 primary labels. \todo{describe second annotator's background; replace all Indian numbers with the post-adjudication run (built\_v4)} Clause selection was partly keyword-assisted and is therefore biased toward typical wording.

\textbf{Batch 2 (106 clauses, 2 documents).} The CPWD General Conditions of Contract 2023 (text extracted from PDF, with some garbled lines) and a draft waste-to-compost concession agreement containing bracketed placeholders. Candidate units were selected by category keywords and labelled by the primary annotator only; no unit was marked as not a clause, so some definitions and noisy units remain. On 53\% of units the label equals the keyword category that selected the unit. This pool has no second-annotator check.

In total, 192 of the 334 clauses fall in categories with CUAD training data (including Other) and 142 in the six unseen categories. Documents are unevenly sized (the largest contributes 67 clauses), and nearly all are government documents.

\textbf{Judgment slice (11 clauses).} Contract clauses quoted verbatim in five Indian court judgments (Indian Kanoon), mostly non-compete, non-solicitation, arbitration and termination clauses. They were hand-picked, are kept separate from the main set, and are scored for classification only.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Model}
The encoder is Longformer-base-4096 \cite{beltagy2020longformer}. The classifier head predicts a category from a clause (maximum 512 tokens). The span head takes a category-specific question and a 3,000-character window of the contract (maximum 1,024 tokens) and predicts start/end logits over the window. An ordinal risk head exists in the code but is not used in any reported result. We compare three variants: classifier only, span only, and joint training. As a classification baseline we fine-tune LEGAL-BERT-base \cite{chalkidis2020legalbert} (\texttt{nlpaueb/legal-bert-base-uncased}, 110M parameters, 512 tokens) as a classifier only, with the same splits and settings; it has no span head.

\subsection{Training and protocol}
All CUAD experiments use contract-grouped 5-fold splits so no contract appears in both training and test data; we ran folds 0--2. Settings: 2 epochs, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ (heads $10^{-4}$), seed 13, mixed precision on a T4 GPU, span training on 50\% of eligible items. Baselines: majority class, TF-IDF with logistic regression (trained on the same split), a whole-window span, a lexical-centroid span, and a random span.

\subsection{Rule layer}
A rule engine applies hand-written features (e.g., whether a duration or cap is stated) to confidently classified clauses and raises candidate flags tied to the Indian Contract Act (ss.\,27, 28, 39, 56, 73--75, 124), the MSMED Act (ss.\,15--16) and the DPDPA (s.\,8). Flags are prompts for human review, not legal findings. The 15 rules have not been reviewed by a lawyer. \todo{legal review of the rules before claiming anything about them}

\section{Results on CUAD}\label{sec:cuad}
Table~\ref{tab:cuad} gives means over three folds (about 2,030 test clauses per fold). The Longformer classifiers beat TF-IDF by 0.036 to 0.037 macro-F1 with fold-level intervals above zero, but the folds are few. Joint training did not change classification (difference $-0.001$, interval includes zero). LEGAL-BERT is the strongest classifier (0.870 macro-F1, +0.031 over the Longformer classifier; its lowest fold, 0.861, is above the Longformer mean), and trains in about 3.4 minutes per fold. Clause classification therefore does not need Longformer's long context; we keep Longformer for the span head. For span localisation, the joint model reaches 0.760 token-F1 against 0.604 for the lexical-centroid baseline.

The same clauses are used for every fold, so the folds are not independent samples. Wilson 95\% intervals on accuracy are roughly $\pm0.07$ and overlap those of TF-IDF in every fold. \textbf{Findings.} (i) Macro-F1 falls from 0.84 on CUAD to about 0.34, consistently across checkpoints. (ii) Out of domain, the classifier is no better than TF-IDF (accuracy 0.344 vs.\ 0.337, macro-F1 0.343 vs.\ 0.345), and neither is LEGAL-BERT (0.349, 0.341) despite its higher CUAD score, so the drop is not specific to the Longformer encoder; LEGAL-BERT sends unseen categories to the same neighbours. (iii) Batch 2 is harder than the original pool (0.275 vs.\ 0.377), but with 63 clauses the intervals overlap (fold 0: 0.16--0.37 vs.\ 0.29--0.45); keyword selection, retained noisy units and PDF noise are possible causes we have not separated. (iv) On Indian text the span head's advantage over the lexical baseline nearly disappears (0.016 token-F1, against 0.156 on CUAD), although its exact match stays well above the baselines. (v) Per category (fold 0), Penalties (F1 0.56) and Termination (0.53) transfer best; Dispute Resolution does not (F1 0.00 on 18 clauses), consistent with its narrow CUAD coverage, and the catch-all Other is mostly misclassified (F1 0.21 on 47). (vi) For the 142 clauses in unseen categories, predictions go to the nearest trained category (fold 0): 21 of 44 Payment clauses become Commercial Terms, 15 of 19 Indemnification clauses become Liability, and 34 of 47 Confidentiality clauses become Other. Per-document accuracy varies (0.14--0.78), but most documents have fewer than 15 clauses in trained categories, so we do not draw conclusions per document.

\textbf{Judgment slice.} On the 11 judgment-quoted clauses, Longformer accuracy is 0.606 (folds: 0.455, 0.727, 0.636), LEGAL-BERT 0.545 and TF-IDF 0.667; with $n=11$ hand-picked clauses, mostly restrictive covenants, this is reported for completeness only.

\section{Span Head versus Classifier Scan}\label{sec:scan}
To locate a clause type in a 3,000-character window one can either run the span head once or split the window into about 50 sentence-aligned candidates and score each with the category classifier. On 500 test windows per fold (T4 GPU), the span head achieves a mean token-F1 of 0.756 against 0.630 for the scan (differences of +0.132, +0.142, +0.102, each with a paired-bootstrap 95\% interval excluding zero), at 54\,ms against about 1,314\,ms per window (about 24$\times$ faster). The two methods use different checkpoints by design, and three folds are too few for a cross-fold interval.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item The Indian set is small (334 clauses, 13 documents, almost all government documents), partly keyword-selected, labelled by one primary annotator (second-annotator $\kappa=0.65$ on 100 units of the original pool; none for batch 2), and partly OCR/PDF-noisy (the airport concession agreement and the CPWD GCC).
\item Only 12 of 18 categories are learnable from CUAD; the system cannot recognise the others.
\item Training was short (2 epochs, 50\% of span items); only 3 of 5 folds and a single seed were run.
\item Folds on the Indian set reuse the same clauses and are not independent.
\item The rule layer is unreviewed by a lawyer; outputs are not legal advice.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Next Steps}\label{sec:next}
A CUAD-trained Longformer loses about 60\% of its macro-F1 on Indian contracts and does not beat TF-IDF there; a LEGAL-BERT classifier that is stronger on CUAD behaves the same. Its span head is fast and accurate on CUAD, but on Indian text it is only marginally better than a lexical baseline.
